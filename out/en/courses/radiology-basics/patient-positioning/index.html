Lesson

Patient Positioning

Duration: 20 minutes

Introduction

Accurate patient positioning is essential for producing diagnostic radiographs. This lesson introduces the basic positioning principles used in X-ray imaging and explains how patient alignment, projection, centering, and anatomical coverage affect the final image.

Lesson Objectives

• Explain the basic principles of patient positioning.

• Understand the difference between common radiographic projections.

• Recognize the importance of patient alignment and anatomical coverage.

• Understand the role of central ray centering.

• Identify common positioning errors.

• Recognize the relationship between positioning and image quality.

Lesson Content

Why Positioning Matters

Correct positioning ensures that the required anatomy is demonstrated clearly and in the appropriate projection. Poor positioning can produce rotation, distortion, anatomy cutoff, or an image that does not answer the clinical question.

Patient Alignment

The patient should be aligned according to the examination and projection being performed. Appropriate alignment helps reduce unwanted rotation and ensures that anatomical structures are represented consistently.

Radiographic Projections

Radiographic projections describe the direction of the X-ray beam through the patient. Common examples include anteroposterior (AP), posteroanterior (PA), and lateral projections. The selected projection depends on the examination and clinical indication.

Central Ray and Centering

The central ray should be directed toward the appropriate anatomical landmark for the examination. Accurate centering helps ensure that the anatomy of interest is included and reduces the risk of unnecessary repeat imaging.

Anatomical Coverage

The required anatomy must be included within the image receptor field. Appropriate positioning and collimation help ensure adequate anatomical coverage while limiting unnecessary exposure to areas outside the region of interest.

Common Positioning Errors

Common errors include patient rotation, incorrect centering, inadequate anatomical coverage, incorrect projection, and poor alignment. Recognizing these errors allows the radiographer to correct technique before repeating an examination.

Practical Example

Checking Positioning Before Exposure

Before making an exposure, the radiographer should confirm patient identification, examination type, projection, anatomical alignment, centering, image receptor placement, and collimation. A short positioning check can prevent avoidable repeat exposures.

Educational Images

Common Mistakes

• Failing to verify the correct projection before exposure.

• Allowing patient rotation to affect the image.

• Incorrectly centering the central ray.

• Excluding required anatomy from the image.

• Using unnecessarily wide collimation.

• Failing to review positioning before repeating an examination.

Key Points

• Correct positioning is essential for diagnostic radiography.

• Patient alignment helps reduce rotation and distortion.

• AP, PA, and lateral are common radiographic projections.

• Accurate central ray centering helps include the required anatomy.

• Appropriate collimation supports both image quality and radiation protection.

• A positioning check before exposure can help prevent repeat examinations.

Takeaway

Accurate patient positioning combines correct alignment, projection, centering, anatomical coverage, and collimation. These principles help produce diagnostic images while reducing avoidable repeat exposures.
