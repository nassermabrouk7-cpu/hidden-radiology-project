Lesson

Chest Anatomy

Duration: 20 minutes

Introduction

Understanding normal chest anatomy is essential for accurate chest X-ray interpretation. This lesson reviews the major anatomical structures visible on a chest radiograph and explains how to recognize their normal appearance and relationships.

Lesson Objectives

• Identify the major anatomical structures visible on a chest X-ray.

• Recognize the lungs, pleura, and central thoracic structures.

• Identify the cardiac silhouette and mediastinal contours.

• Understand the normal appearance of the diaphragms and costophrenic angles.

• Recognize the hilar regions and major pulmonary vessels.

• Develop a structured approach to identifying normal chest anatomy.

Lesson Content

Overview of Chest Anatomy on X-Ray

A chest X-ray provides a two-dimensional representation of complex three-dimensional thoracic anatomy. Accurate interpretation requires familiarity with the normal position, shape, density, and relationships of the major thoracic structures.

Lungs and Lung Fields

The lung fields are normally relatively radiolucent because they contain air. The pulmonary vascular markings are visible throughout the lungs and normally become less prominent toward the periphery. Both lungs should be compared for symmetry in size, density, and vascular pattern.

Pleura and Costophrenic Angles

The pleura is usually not directly visible on a normal chest X-ray except where pleural surfaces meet or become outlined by adjacent structures. The costophrenic angles should normally appear sharp. Blunting may indicate pleural fluid or another pleural abnormality.

Hila and Pulmonary Vessels

The hila contain the main pulmonary vessels, bronchi, lymphatic structures, and surrounding soft tissues. The left hilum is commonly positioned slightly higher than the right. The hilar contours and vascular distribution should be assessed for symmetry and abnormal enlargement.

Heart and Cardiac Silhouette

The cardiac silhouette is formed primarily by the heart chambers and adjacent great vessels. On a properly positioned PA chest radiograph, the transverse cardiac diameter is normally less than half the internal thoracic diameter. The cardiac borders should be assessed systematically for contour and size.

Mediastinum

The mediastinum contains the heart, great vessels, trachea, esophagus, lymphatic structures, and other soft tissues. The superior mediastinum and aortic contour should be evaluated for width, shape, and symmetry. Abnormal contour changes may indicate underlying pathology.

Diaphragm

The diaphragms form the inferior boundaries of the thoracic cavities. The right hemidiaphragm is normally slightly higher than the left because of the liver. The diaphragmatic contours should be smooth, and both costophrenic angles should remain clearly visible when there is no pleural abnormality.

Trachea and Main Bronchi

The trachea should normally appear as a central or near-central radiolucent air column. It divides at the carina into the right and left main bronchi. Tracheal deviation or abnormal narrowing can provide important diagnostic information.

Bones and Soft Tissues

The ribs, clavicles, scapulae, vertebral bodies, and other visible bones should be reviewed as part of every chest X-ray interpretation. The soft tissues of the chest wall and neck should also be assessed for asymmetry, abnormal swelling, or subcutaneous abnormalities.

Normal Anatomical Relationships

Chest X-ray interpretation depends not only on identifying individual structures but also on understanding their normal relationships. The cardiac silhouette, hila, mediastinum, diaphragm, and lung fields should be evaluated together to recognize subtle deviations from normal anatomy.

Practical Anatomy Checklist

A useful anatomical checklist includes the trachea, hila, lung fields, pleural spaces, costophrenic angles, cardiac silhouette, mediastinum, diaphragms, bones, and surrounding soft tissues. Reviewing these structures consistently helps reduce missed findings.

Key Points

• Normal chest anatomy must be understood before interpreting pathology.

• The lungs are predominantly radiolucent because they contain air.

• The left hilum is normally slightly higher than the right.

• The cardiac silhouette should be assessed for size and contour.

• The right hemidiaphragm is normally slightly higher than the left.

• Sharp costophrenic angles are a normal finding.

• The trachea should be assessed for position and patency.

• Bones and soft tissues are part of every complete chest X-ray review.
